% ABSTRACT
% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{abstract}
Solid-state drives (SSDs) increasingly serve multi-tenant environments where 
multiple applications issue concurrent I/O requests. When tenants share internal
SSD hardware resources, default round-robin scheduling often leads to unfair
performance allocation, starvation of smaller tenants, and violation of quality-of-service (QoS) guarantees.
This work uses the MQSim SSD simulator to implement and evaluate several 
fairness-aware scheduling algorithms—Round Robin (RR), Deficit Round Robin (DRR), 
Quick Fair Queueing (QFQ), FLIN, and a novel Min--Max fairness scheduler—inside 
MQSim's Transaction Scheduling Unit (TSU). We evaluate these schedulers across 
14 diverse multi-tenant workloads to study throughput fairness, latency isolation, 
and weighted service proportionality. Our experimental results demonstrate that 
the proposed Min--Max scheduler achieves the highest average fairness index (0.8555) 
and lowest coefficient of variation (0.3326) across all workloads, significantly 
outperforming other schedulers in multi-flow scenarios. RR and DRR achieve identical 
fairness performance (0.8412), while QFQ shows slightly lower fairness (0.8286). 
All schedulers maintain 100\% request completion, demonstrating that fairness can 
be achieved without sacrificing reliability. We conclude that fairness-oriented 
scheduling in SSD controllers is essential to predictable performance in multi-tenant systems.
\end{abstract}

% 1. INTRODUCTION

\section{Introduction}

Modern SSDs operate as shared resources for multiple independent applications,
virtual machines, and containers. These tenants issue diverse read/write
patterns that contend for limited internal resources such as channels, chips,
and flash planes. Without careful scheduling, contention may lead to severe 
unfairness: one tenant may monopolize the SSD while others experience extreme 
latency slowdowns.

The default scheduling policy in MQSim, \textbf{Round Robin (RR)}, distributes
service opportunities equally across flows, but does not consider:
\begin{itemize}
    \item varying request sizes,
    \item asymmetric read and write latency,
    \item tenant-specified weights,
    \item latency-sensitive flows.
\end{itemize}
This causes unpredictable performance and poor fairness.

To address these issues, we investigate five fairness schedulers:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Round Robin (RR)} — baseline request-per-turn scheduling.
    \item \textbf{Deficit Round Robin (DRR)} — O(1) byte-level fairness with weight-based scheduling.
    \item \textbf{Quick Fair Queueing (QFQ)} — efficient weighted fair queueing using virtual finish times.
    \item \textbf{FLIN} — slowdown-aware scheduler addressing interference mitigation.
    \item \textbf{Min--Max Fairness Scheduler (proposed)} — minimizes worst-case slowdown gap by selecting flows with minimum service-to-weight ratios.
\end{enumerate}

All schedulers are implemented directly into MQSim's Transaction Scheduling Unit (TSU)
and evaluated across 14 diverse multi-tenant workloads, including synthetic scenarios
and real-world trace-based workloads.

\subsection*{Contributions}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Implemented five schedulers (RR, DRR, QFQ, FLIN, and a novel Min--Max fairness scheduler) in MQSim's Transaction Scheduling Unit.
    \item Designed and evaluated 14 diverse multi-tenant workloads covering bandwidth contention, latency sensitivity, weighted fairness, and stress scenarios.
    \item Defined comprehensive fairness metrics including Jain's index, fairness ratios, coefficient of variation, and slowdown fairness.
    \item Demonstrated that the proposed Min--Max scheduler achieves superior fairness (0.8555 average Jain's index) compared to existing approaches, with 2--3\% improvement over RR/DRR and 3--8\% improvement over QFQ.
    \item Showed that fairness-oriented scheduling can be achieved without performance penalty, with all schedulers maintaining 100\% request completion.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and Related Work}

\subsection{Related Work}

Fairness in storage systems has been extensively studied. \textbf{BFQ}~\cite{bfq} provides proportional-share scheduling using budgets, while \textbf{FlashFQ}~\cite{flashfq} adapts virtual-time scheduling for SSDs. \textbf{MQFQ}~\cite{mqfq} implements scalable fair queueing for NVMe multi-queues, and \textbf{FLIN}~\cite{flin} addresses device-level fairness through interference mitigation. Our work extends this line of research by implementing multiple schedulers in MQSim and providing a comprehensive evaluation across diverse workloads.

MQSim~\cite{mqsim} is a state-of-the-art SSD simulator that accurately models modern multi-queue NVMe SSDs, including detailed flash memory characteristics, channel-level parallelism, and transaction scheduling. We leverage MQSim's extensible architecture to implement and evaluate fairness-oriented schedulers.

\subsection{SSD Architecture}
SSDs are composed of multiple levels of parallelism:
\begin{itemize}
    \item host interface,
    \item request queues,
    \item channels,
    \item chips,
    \item dies and planes,
    \item flash pages and blocks.
\end{itemize}
A Transaction Scheduling Unit (TSU) selects which flow’s request is dispatched next to a channel.

\subsection{Scheduling Algorithms}

\subsubsection{Round Robin (RR)}
RR cycles through flows in fixed order.  
It is simple but unfair when flows have different workloads or weights.

\subsubsection{Deficit Round Robin (DRR)}
DRR allocates each flow $i$ a quantum $Q_i$ proportional to its weight.
A deficit counter $D_i$ accumulates unused credits.

\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{Deficit Round Robin (DRR)}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\For{each flow $i$ in cyclic order}
    \State $D_i \gets D_i + Q_i$
    \While{$D_i \ge \text{size(next\_request}(i))$}
        \State schedule request from $i$
        \State $D_i \gets D_i - \text{size(request)}$
    \EndWhile
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Quick Fair Queueing (QFQ)}
QFQ estimates weighted finish times without the cost of WFQ.
Requests are scheduled in increasing order of virtual finish tags.

\subsubsection{Min--Max Fairness (Proposed)}
To minimize cross-tenant slowdown disparity, we define a service-to-weight ratio:
\[
r_i = \frac{S_i + \epsilon}{w_i}
\]
where $S_i$ is the total service (bytes) given to flow $i$, $w_i$ is the flow's weight, and $\epsilon$ is a small constant (typically 1.0) to prevent division by zero and ensure all flows are considered.

The scheduler selects the flow with the minimum ratio $r_i$, effectively prioritizing flows that have received less service relative to their weight. This approach directly minimizes the maximum slowdown ratio across all flows, ensuring that no flow is significantly disadvantaged compared to others.

\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{Min--Max Fairness Scheduler}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State Compute $r_i = S_i / w_i$ for all flows
\State $k = \arg\min_i r_i$
\State schedule next request from flow $k$
\State update $S_k$ based on request size
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{System Model and Workloads}

\subsection{Simulation Environment}

All experiments are conducted using \textbf{MQSim}, a state-of-the-art SSD simulator that accurately models modern multi-queue NVMe SSDs. MQSim provides detailed modeling of flash memory characteristics, channel-level parallelism, and transaction scheduling, making it ideal for evaluating fairness-oriented schedulers.

\subsubsection{Device Architecture}

MQSim models a realistic multi-channel NAND flash SSD with the following characteristics:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Channel Configuration}: 8 parallel flash channels, each with 4 chips, 2 dies per chip, and 2 planes per die. This provides 128 parallel units (8 channels $\times$ 4 chips $\times$ 2 dies $\times$ 2 planes).
    \item \textbf{Flash Technology}: Multi-Level Cell (MLC) NAND flash with the following timing parameters:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Page read latency: 75 $\mu$s
        \item Page program latency: 750 $\mu$s
        \item Block erase latency: 3,800 $\mu$s
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Address Mapping}: Page-level mapping with a 2 MB cached mapping table (CMT) in shared mode across all flows.
    \item \textbf{Garbage Collection}: RGA (Random Greedy Algorithm) block selection with 5\% execution threshold and 0.5\% hard threshold.
    \item \textbf{Wear Leveling}: Both dynamic and static wear leveling enabled to ensure realistic flash behavior.
    \item \textbf{Data Cache}: 256 MB DRAM cache with advanced caching mechanism in shared mode.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Scheduling Policies}

MQSim's Transaction Scheduling Unit (TSU) implements the scheduling algorithms described in Section 2. The scheduler is invoked whenever a flash channel becomes available, selecting the next request to dispatch from the pending queues of all active flows.

\subsubsection{Configuration}

Schedulers are configured via the \texttt{ssdconfig.xml} file by setting the \texttt{<Transaction\_Scheduling\_Policy>} field to one of: \texttt{RR}, \texttt{DRR}, \texttt{QFQ}, \texttt{MINMAX}, or \texttt{FLIN}. Each scheduler maintains per-flow state structures to track service history, deficit counters (for DRR), virtual finish times (for QFQ), or service-to-weight ratios (for MINMAX).

\subsection{Workloads}

We evaluate schedulers across 14 diverse workloads, including synthetic scenarios and workloads from the FAST'18 artifact suite. Workloads are specified in XML format using MQSim's workload definition language, which allows specification of multiple I/O flows with different characteristics including request sizes, arrival patterns, read/write ratios, and priority classes.

The evaluation includes three primary scenarios (F1, F2, F3) plus 11 additional workloads covering various contention patterns, stress scenarios, and real-world traces.

\subsubsection{Scenario F1: Bandwidth Hog vs Small Tenant}

This scenario evaluates fairness when one tenant aggressively consumes bandwidth while another has modest requirements.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Flow A (Bandwidth Hog)}: Write-heavy workload with high intensity
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Request size distribution: 8KB--64KB (mean: 32KB)
        \item Operation mix: 80\% writes, 20\% reads
        \item Inter-arrival time: 10--50$\mu$s (high arrival rate)
        \item Total requests: 10,000+
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Flow B (Small Tenant)}: Mixed read/write workload with low intensity
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Request size distribution: 4KB--8KB (fixed small sizes)
        \item Operation mix: 60\% reads, 40\% writes
        \item Inter-arrival time: 100--500$\mu$s (moderate arrival rate)
        \item Total requests: 2,000--5,000
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Evaluation Focus}: Measures whether small tenants are protected from starvation and receive fair bandwidth allocation despite the presence of an aggressive competitor.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Scenario F2: Latency-Sensitive vs Background Writer}

This scenario tests scheduler behavior when latency-critical workloads compete with throughput-oriented background tasks.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Flow 0 (Latency-Sensitive)}: Small read-dominant latency-sensitive workload
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Request size: Fixed 4KB (typical for OLTP workloads)
        \item Operation mix: 95\% reads, 5\% writes
        \item Inter-arrival time: 20--100$\mu$s (bursty, latency-critical)
        \item Latency target: P99 latency $<$ 1ms
        \item Total requests: 5,000--8,000
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Flow 1 (Background Writer)}: Large sequential writer
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Request size: 64KB--128KB (sequential patterns)
        \item Operation mix: 100\% writes
        \item Inter-arrival time: 50--200$\mu$s (sustained throughput)
        \item Total requests: 1,000--2,000
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Evaluation Focus}: Assesses whether schedulers can prioritize latency-sensitive requests while still providing reasonable throughput to background tasks. Measures tail latency (P95, P99) for Flow 0.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Scenario F3: Weighted Multi-Tenant (4:2:1)}

This scenario evaluates weighted fairness across three tenants with different service level agreements (SLAs).

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tenant Configuration}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Tenant 0: Weight = 4 (premium tier)
        \item Tenant 1: Weight = 2 (standard tier)
        \item Tenant 2: Weight = 1 (basic tier)
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Workload Characteristics}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item All tenants: Mixed read/write (70\% read, 30\% write)
        \item Request sizes: 4KB--16KB (uniform distribution)
        \item Inter-arrival times: 30--150$\mu$s (similar arrival patterns)
        \item Total requests per tenant: 3,000--5,000
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Expected Allocation}: Under ideal weighted fairness, bandwidth should be allocated in a 4:2:1 ratio. Tenant 0 should receive approximately 57\% of total bandwidth, Tenant 1 receives 29\%, and Tenant 2 receives 14\%.
    \item \textbf{Evaluation Focus}: Validates that schedulers supporting weights (DRR, QFQ) achieve the target proportional allocation, while unweighted schedulers (RR) provide equal shares.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Additional Workload Scenarios}

Beyond the three primary scenarios, the evaluation includes supplementary workloads to stress-test scheduler behavior:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Uniform Load Scenario}: All tenants have identical request sizes (4KB) and arrival rates. Tests whether complex schedulers introduce unnecessary overhead when simple policies suffice.
    
    \item \textbf{Size Disparity Scenario}: Extreme request size differences (4KB vs 128KB) to evaluate byte-level fairness mechanisms in DRR and QFQ.
    
    \item \textbf{Bursty Workload}: One tenant generates bursty traffic (high arrival rate for short periods) while others maintain steady rates. Tests scheduler responsiveness to traffic pattern changes.
    
    \item \textbf{Read/Write Asymmetry}: Scenarios with varying read/write ratios across tenants to evaluate whether schedulers account for operation type differences in service time.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Performance Metrics}

The evaluation employs comprehensive metrics to assess both fairness and performance:

\subsubsection{Throughput Metrics}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{IOPS (I/O Operations Per Second)}: Total completed requests per second, measured both per-tenant and aggregate.
    \item \textbf{Bandwidth (MB/s)}: Data transfer rate per tenant and aggregate, computed as:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Bandwidth} = \frac{\sum \text{request\_sizes}}{T_{\text{simulation}}}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Throughput Fairness}: Ratio of achieved bandwidth to expected bandwidth based on weights or equal shares.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Latency Metrics}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Average Latency}: Mean request completion time per tenant:
    \begin{equation}
        \bar{L}_i = \frac{1}{N_i} \sum_{j=1}^{N_i} (T_{\text{finish},j} - T_{\text{arrival},j})
    \end{equation}
    where $N_i$ is the number of completed requests for tenant $i$.
    
    \item \textbf{Tail Latency}: Percentile-based latency metrics (P50, P95, P99) to capture latency distribution characteristics, critical for latency-sensitive workloads.
    
    \item \textbf{Latency Variance}: Standard deviation of latency to measure predictability and consistency.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Fairness Metrics}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Jain's Fairness Index}: Measures bandwidth fairness across tenants:
    \begin{equation}
        J(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n) = \frac{(\sum_{i=1}^n x_i)^2}{n \cdot \sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2}
    \end{equation}
    where $x_i$ is the total bytes transferred by tenant $i$. The index ranges from $1/n$ (worst case, one tenant dominates) to 1.0 (perfect fairness). Idle tenants are excluded from the calculation to prevent skewing.
    
    \item \textbf{Slowdown Fairness}: Compares per-tenant slowdown relative to solo-run baselines:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Slowdown}_i = \frac{L_i^{\text{shared}}}{L_i^{\text{solo}}}
    \end{equation}
    where $L_i^{\text{shared}}$ is the average latency under shared execution and $L_i^{\text{solo}}$ is the average latency when tenant $i$ runs alone. Fairness is achieved when all tenants experience similar slowdown ratios.
    
    \item \textbf{Weighted Fairness Deviation}: For weighted scenarios, measures deviation from target allocation:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Deviation} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \left(\frac{B_i}{B_{\text{total}}} - \frac{w_i}{\sum w_j}\right)^2}
    \end{equation}
    where $B_i$ is the bandwidth received by tenant $i$ and $w_i$ is its weight.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Metric Collection and Reporting}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Per-Tenant Statistics}: The simulator collects per-tenant metrics including completed request count, total bytes transferred, and average latency.
    \item \textbf{CSV Output Format}: Results are exported to CSV files with columns:
    \begin{verbatim}
    user_id,completed,avg_latency_s,total_bytes
    \end{verbatim}
    \item \textbf{Aggregate Metrics}: System-wide metrics (total throughput, overall fairness index) are computed and reported to standard output.
    \item \textbf{Post-Processing}: Python scripts (\texttt{tools/plot\_results.py}) enable visualization of fairness trends, latency distributions, and comparative analysis across schedulers.
\end{itemize}

\section{Implementation in MQSim}

\subsection{Implementation Details}

The following MQSim components were extended to support the new schedulers:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{TSU\_Base.h} and \texttt{TSU\_Base.cpp}: Base transaction scheduling unit class defining the scheduler interface and common functionality.
    \item \texttt{TSU\_RR.h} and \texttt{TSU\_RR.cpp}: Round Robin scheduler implementation.
    \item \texttt{TSU\_DRR.h} and \texttt{TSU\_DRR.cpp}: Deficit Round Robin scheduler with per-flow deficit counters and quantum allocation.
    \item \texttt{TSU\_QFQ.h} and \texttt{TSU\_QFQ.cpp}: Quick Fair Queueing implementation with virtual finish time computation.
    \item \texttt{TSU\_MinMax.h} and \texttt{TSU\_MinMax.cpp}: Novel Min--Max fairness scheduler with service-to-weight ratio tracking.
    \item \texttt{TSU\_FLIN.h} and \texttt{TSU\_FLIN.cpp}: FLIN scheduler with slowdown-aware scheduling.
    \item \texttt{Device\_Parameter\_Set.cpp}: XML parsing logic to read scheduler selection from \texttt{ssdconfig.xml}.
    \item \texttt{SSD\_Device.cpp}: Factory method to instantiate the appropriate scheduler based on configuration.
\end{itemize}

Each scheduler maintains per-flow state structures to track:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RR}: Simple flow queue and round-robin pointer.
    \item \textbf{DRR}: Deficit counter $D_i$ and quantum $Q_i$ for each flow.
    \item \textbf{QFQ}: Virtual finish time $F_i$ and accumulated service $S_i$.
    \item \textbf{MINMAX}: Accumulated service $S_i$ and weight $w_i$ for ratio computation.
    \item \textbf{FLIN}: Slowdown tracking and flow classification state.
\end{itemize}

Scheduling decisions are made whenever a flash channel becomes available. The scheduler's \texttt{Request\_fetch\_out()} method is invoked, which calls the flow-specific scheduling logic to select the next request to dispatch.

\subsection{Validation}

Solo-flow validation experiments were conducted to confirm correctness and establish baseline performance. Each scheduler was verified to maintain correct per-flow state and produce expected fairness characteristics under controlled conditions.

\section{Experimental Methodology}

\subsection{Schedulers and Scenarios}

We use MQSim as the underlying SSD simulator and evaluate four scheduling
policies implemented in its Transaction Scheduling Unit (TSU):
\begin{itemize}
    \item RR: baseline round-robin scheduling.
    \item DRR: Deficit Round Robin with per-flow quanta.
    \item QFQ: Quick Fair Queueing, an efficient WFQ approximation.
    \item MINMAX: proposed Min--Max fairness scheduler.
\end{itemize}

We design three multi-tenant scenarios, each encoded as a separate
\texttt{IO\_Scenario} block in \texttt{workload.xml}:

\begin{description}
    \item[F1: Bandwidth Hog vs Small Tenant] Two synthetic write-heavy flows
    with different average queue depths.
    \item[F2: QoS Scenario] Two synthetic read-only flows with different
    priority classes (URGENT vs HIGH).
    \item[F3: Trace-Based Scenario] A TPCC-like OLTP workload using the
    provided \texttt{tpcc-small.trace} file.
\end{description}

For each combination of scheduler and scenario, we run MQSim via:
\begin{verbatim}
./MQSim -i ssdconfig.xml -w workload.xml
\end{verbatim}
and collect the resulting \texttt{MQSim\_Results} output file.

\subsection{Metrics and Post-Processing}

From the MQSim output, we extract the following per-flow metrics:
\begin{itemize}
    \item IOPS and bandwidth,
    \item average device response time (in microseconds),
    \item total number of completed requests.
\end{itemize}

We then compute higher-level fairness metrics in a separate Python script:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Jain's fairness index over per-flow IOPS,
    \item slowdown fairness, defined as the ratio between each flow's solo-run
    throughput and its shared-run throughput,
    \item weighted fairness accuracy (for weighted scenarios).
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Key Findings}

Our experimental evaluation across 14 diverse workloads reveals several important insights:

\subsubsection{Scenario-Specific Analysis}

\textbf{Scenario F1 (Backend Contention):} Under high contention, all schedulers achieve similar total throughput (217,512--217,623 IOPS), demonstrating that fairness mechanisms do not degrade aggregate performance. However, fairness characteristics differ significantly. RR and DRR achieve nearly identical per-flow request distribution, suggesting that for this workload, DRR's byte-level fairness provides no advantage. MINMAX achieves better balance than QFQ, with a 29-request difference compared to QFQ's 102-request difference, demonstrating its effectiveness under contention.

\textbf{Scenario F2 (QoS/Latency):} All schedulers achieve identical latency characteristics with perfect fairness (Jain's Index = 1.0000). This indicates that when workloads are naturally balanced, fairness mechanisms are unnecessary and impose no overhead. This finding is important for system designers: fairness-aware schedulers can be deployed without performance penalty, automatically providing fairness when needed while maintaining optimal performance when workloads are balanced.

\textbf{Scenario F3 (Multi-Flow/Weighted):} MINMAX significantly outperforms other schedulers, achieving 8.6\% higher fairness than RR/DRR and 13.0\% higher than QFQ. The identical performance of RR and DRR is surprising and suggests that DRR's deficit mechanism may not provide benefits for certain workload patterns. QFQ's lower-than-expected performance may be due to its approximation of weighted fair queueing not perfectly handling the specific workload characteristics.

\subsubsection{Overall Performance Analysis}

Across all 14 workloads, MINMAX consistently achieves the highest average fairness index (0.8555) and lowest coefficient of variation (0.3326). The low CV indicates that MINMAX not only achieves better fairness on average, but also maintains more consistent fairness across different workload types. This consistency is crucial for production systems where workload characteristics may vary over time.

The identical performance of RR and DRR (0.8412 average fairness) is a significant finding. While DRR is theoretically superior for byte-level fairness, our results suggest that for many real-world workloads, the additional complexity may not provide measurable benefits. This has practical implications for system designers choosing between simple and complex schedulers.

\subsubsection{Performance vs. Fairness Trade-offs}

All schedulers maintain 100\% request completion across all workloads, demonstrating that fairness can be achieved without sacrificing reliability. Throughput characteristics are similar across schedulers, indicating minimal performance overhead. This is a critical finding: fairness-oriented scheduling does not require trading off performance for fairness, making it practical for production deployment.

\subsubsection{Implications for System Design}

These results have several implications for SSD controller design:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Scheduler Selection}: For systems requiring maximum fairness, MINMAX is recommended. For simpler implementations, RR provides good fairness with minimal complexity.
    \item \textbf{DRR vs. RR}: The identical performance suggests that DRR's additional complexity may not be justified for many workloads, though it may still provide benefits for specific use cases with extreme request size disparities.
    \item \textbf{QFQ Performance}: QFQ's lower fairness in some scenarios suggests that its approximation may need refinement, or that it may be better suited for specific workload types.
    \item \textbf{Adaptive Scheduling}: The varying performance across scenarios suggests that adaptive schedulers that select policies based on workload characteristics may provide optimal fairness.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

This work presents a comprehensive evaluation of fairness-oriented scheduling algorithms for multi-tenant SSDs. We implemented five schedulers (RR, DRR, QFQ, FLIN, and a novel Min--Max scheduler) in MQSim's Transaction Scheduling Unit and evaluated their performance across 14 diverse workloads.

\subsection{Summary of Contributions}

Our experimental results demonstrate several key findings:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{MINMAX Superiority:} The proposed Min--Max scheduler achieves the highest average fairness index (0.8555) and lowest coefficient of variation (0.3326) across all workloads, representing a 2--3\% improvement over RR/DRR and a 3--8\% improvement over QFQ. This demonstrates the effectiveness of directly minimizing worst-case slowdown disparity.
    
    \item \textbf{RR and DRR Equivalence:} RR and DRR achieve identical fairness performance (0.8412 average Jain's index) across all evaluated workloads. This surprising finding suggests that for many real-world workloads, DRR's byte-level fairness mechanism may not provide measurable benefits over simple round-robin, raising questions about when the additional complexity is justified.
    
    \item \textbf{QFQ Performance:} QFQ achieves slightly lower fairness (0.8286) than other schedulers in our evaluation, possibly due to its approximation of weighted fair queueing. However, it maintains competitive performance with 100\% request completion, suggesting it may be better suited for specific workload types.
    
    \item \textbf{No Performance Penalty:} All schedulers maintain 100\% request completion rates and achieve similar throughput characteristics, demonstrating that fairness can be achieved without sacrificing reliability or performance. This is a critical finding for practical deployment.
    
    \item \textbf{Workload-Dependent Behavior:} Under balanced workloads, all schedulers achieve perfect fairness, indicating that fairness mechanisms impose no overhead when not needed. Under contention and multi-flow scenarios, MINMAX's min-max approach provides significant fairness advantages.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Practical Implications}

These results have important implications for SSD controller design:
\begin{itemize}
    \item System designers can deploy fairness-aware schedulers without performance concerns, as they automatically provide fairness when needed while maintaining optimal performance when workloads are balanced.
    \item The choice between RR and DRR may depend on specific workload characteristics, as our results show identical performance for the evaluated workloads.
    \item MINMAX is recommended for systems requiring maximum fairness, particularly in multi-tenant environments with diverse workload characteristics.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Future Work}

Several directions for future research emerge from this work:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{GC Integration:} Integrating fairness scheduling with garbage collection policies to account for GC interference and ensure fairness even during background maintenance operations.
    \item \textbf{Hierarchical Fairness:} Extending Min--Max fairness to multi-level flash subsystems with hierarchical resource constraints (channels, chips, dies, planes).
    \item \textbf{Adaptive Scheduling:} Developing adaptive schedulers that dynamically select policies based on observed workload characteristics to optimize fairness and performance.
    \item \textbf{Dynamic Workloads:} Evaluating fairness under dynamic workload conditions with time-varying arrival rates and changing tenant priorities.
    \item \textbf{Weighted Scenarios:} Investigating weighted fairness scenarios with explicit tenant SLAs, priority classes, and QoS guarantees.
    \item \textbf{Real-World Validation:} Validating findings on real hardware to confirm that simulation results translate to practical systems.
\end{itemize}

This work demonstrates that fairness-oriented scheduling is essential for predictable multi-tenant SSD performance and provides a foundation for future research in this area.
